# "Trackflation:" The NCAA is rapidly getting faster, but not at the same rate #

The phenomenon of "trackflation," unknown to anyone outside of the world of running but heavily discussed by most inside it, is fairly simple: runners have been getting rapidly faster in the past decade. This is seen on all levels: the fastest high schoolers, collegians and professionals are upping the standards in their respective events.

*(NOTE: I chose the NCAA because it has the most readily available data. I analyzed indoor times because the NCAA indoor track season is currently happening and therefore the most relevant. All of my data was manually collected, so for the sake of time and simplicity, I only analyzed men's times from the last 15 years, and I only picked three events: a pure sprint (60 meters), a mid-distance event (1 mile), and a long-distance event (5,000 meters).*

However, this trend is not happening at an even rate, as my graphs show. In all three of the events graphed, the average of the top ten men's indoor times in the NCAA has dropped in the last 15 years, but the 5,000 meters has experienced the most significant of those drops, while the mile has seen a less aggressive drop and the 60 meters has seen the least aggressive drop.

These differences are not random -- they illustrate what factors have the biggest influence on performance. 

All of these events were influenced by rule changes that granted more years of eligibility to student-athletes, as well as the growth of foreign recruiting (which created a wider talent pool). 

However, one of the major developments in the past decade of running has been the growth of "super shoes" and "super spikes," which are widely shown to improve distance running times. These shoes have carbon plates and special foams that increase stiffness and rigidity and improve energy return, which is the efficiency of the midsole in returning stored energy to the runner. The longer the event, the more of an impact these shoes are going to have, which is why the 5,000 meters has had the most dramatic improvement in average times.

There is clearly an inflection point around 2020 in each graph, and this aligns with the release of the Nike Dragonfly, the first "super spike," in 2020. These spikes were first widely worn by collegiate athletes in the 2021 indoor season, and times rapidly dropped from there.

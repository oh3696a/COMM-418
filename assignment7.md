# Understanding the tree disparity in Washington, D.C. #

[Link to graphics](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1GR3dHPwPaQ3p2nb9xVdSZ4bxkvdt5QYShh2RNkJ3FlI/edit?usp=sharing)

There is a clear positive association between the wealth of a Washington, D.C. ward and its tree canopy coverage, as shown by an analysis of tree coverage in the district.

However, as these visualizations show, that association is far from perfect, and there are several confounding variables that complicate this analysis. Figure 1 shows that with a surface-level analysis, there appears to be almost no correlation; some of the wealthiest Wards in the district -- Wards 1, 2 and 6 -- have some of the least tree coverage.

This is where land usage comes into play. Wards 1, 2 and 6 have the three lowest percentages of single-unit housing (out of all housing) in the district, and Wards 2 and 6 contain a lot of special-use land -- like the National Mall -- where there is little tree coverage by design. Ward 5, which contains about half of the district's industrialized land, is also an outlier.

If these four Wards are eliminated and the other four Wards (3, 4, 7 and 8) are analyzed, a clearer pattern emerges: in primarily residential areas, more wealth equals more trees.

This is shown in three different ways in Figures 3, 6 and 7. Using three different variables for wealth -- per capita income, household income and average owner-occupied unit value -- each visualization essentially leads to the same conclusion: Wards 3, 4, 7 and 8 follow the same correlation, while the other four Wards are, to an extent, outliers. 

Why does this matter? Tree coverage is directly linked to positive health outcomes. Trees can help reduce the temperature of urban areas, reduce the need for air conditioning and remove pollutants from the air.

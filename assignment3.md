# Can you buy tree cover in Washington, D.C.? #
Prospective residents of Washington, D.C. who want tree coverage in their neighborhoods may find that it comes at a cost.

A new analysis of tree location data in the district shows that in primarily residential areas, higher tree canopy coverage is associated with higher per capita income. Wards 3 and 4, which have per capita incomes of $111,061 and $71,256, respectively, feature some of the highest tree coverage in the district -- Ward 3 has 54,575 trees per square mile, while Ward 4 is close behind at 48,033.

Meanwhile, Wards 5, 7 and 8 -- the three least affluent in the district -- are lacking in coverage; they average 26,490 trees per square mile, less than half of what Ward 3 has to offer.

However, the data isn't perfectly neat. Wards 1, 2 and 6 buck the trend; all three have a per capita income of over $90,000, but none of them have more than 25,000 trees per square mile. Why do some of the wealthiest parts of the district have the least tree coverage?

Most neighborhoods in these three wards have a lot of commercial or specialized land. Ward 2, for example, features the Downtown, Penn Quarter and West End neighborhoods -- what most people would consider DC's central business district -- as well as the National Mall. Ward 6, meanwhile, is where Capitol Hill and the Southwest Waterfront lie. Trees don't often appear in these areas, and it's not for a lack of wealth.

The same thinking can be applied to other wards. Ward 4, for example, has a relatively modest per capita income (still significantly higher than Wards 5, 7 and 8) but similar canopy coverage to the wealthier Ward 3; this is due at least somewhat to Rock Creek Park, which is almost entirely in Ward 4. Advisory Neighborhood Commission 3/4G (split between Wards 3 and 4 but mostly belonging to 4), which features a large portion of the park, has over 100,000 trees per mile, while nearby ANC 4D has only about 17,000. 

In areas where  single-unit housing is more prominent, the trend is pronounced: more wealth means more trees.

## What does this mean? ##

Tree canopy coverage means more than making a neighborhood look pretty. There are [plenty of tangible benefits](https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/benefits-trees-and-vegetation) to being in proximity to more trees -- economically, environmentally and socially.

Trees provide natural shade and can reduce the heat island effect, leading to reduced air conditioning needs and, in turn, lower electric bills. They also remove pollutants from the air, lowering an area's air quality index.

On the social side, trees have been shown to make people happier and lead to a higher quality of life.

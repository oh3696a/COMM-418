# Trees are not spread equally in Washington, D.C. Here's why. #

By Oliver Hinson

[Link to Sheets](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1GR3dHPwPaQ3p2nb9xVdSZ4bxkvdt5QYShh2RNkJ3FlI/edit?usp=sharing)

Money can buy tree coverage in Washington, D.C. — to an extent — according to an analysis of tree locations in the district.

Using 2023 [data](https://opendata.dc.gov/datasets/08bda06465a74cd59a6d4231d882588d_11/explore?location=38.900041%2C-76.979125%2C16) from the District Department of Transportation’s Urban Forestry Division and 2022 Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) data to estimate tree locations, as well as demographic data from the 2010 census, the analysis shows a positive association between the wealth of a Ward or census tract in D.C. and its number of trees per square mile.

That association is far from perfect, though. Wealth is just one factor in a complex picture of tree coverage inequality in the district.

Ward 3 most closely follows the association. As of the 2010 census, Ward 3 was the wealthiest in the district with a per capita income of $111,061, and it also boasts the highest number of trees per square mile at just under 55,000.

Ward 8, meanwhile, is the poorest in the district with a per capita income of $37,065, and it has less than half of Ward 3’s tree coverage at just over 26,000 per square mile.

Other wards do not follow this pattern. Wards 1, 2 and 6 are the three wealthiest behind Ward 3, but they have the least tree coverage.

This is partly because of land usage. Wards 1, 2 and 6 have the three lowest percentages of single-unit housing (as a percentage of all housing), and they heavily feature downtown, mixed-use and special purpose land. Ward 2 is also dominated by the National Mall, a massive green space that features few trees.

Ward 4 is an outlier in the other direction. It sits in the middle of the pack with a per capita income of a little over $70,000, but it boasts over 48,000 trees per square mile, only a few thousand behind Ward 3 and 15,000 higher than any other ward. 

This is largely due to Rock Creek Park, a concentrated supply of hundreds of thousands of trees. The vast majority of Ward 4’s census tracts contain about 20,000 trees per square mile, but two of the largest tracts in the ward contain over 80,000 per square mile, boosting the average.

## The legacy of urban planning ##

The history of Washington, D.C.’s urban planning also tells a story when it comes to tree coverage. 

Corey Shaw Jr., the historian for grassroots organization [Empower DC](https://www.empowerdc.org/), says that while most people know about the 1791 city plan designed by Pierre L’Enfant, few know about the impact of another urban planner: Harland Bartholomew. 

Bartholomew developed comprehensive plans for dozens of U.S. cities, and he became infamous for using his plans to perpetuate racial segregation.

“Harland Bartholomew needs to be an infamous name amongst city planners and community organizers,” Shaw said. “In every place that he went, he mapped out black areas and then inundated them with heavy industry.”

In 1920, he developed D.C.’s first zoning ordinance, and he selected what is now Ward 5 to have much of the district’s industrial land. That plan never changed substantially, Shaw says, which is why neighborhoods like Ivy City have a lack of tree coverage today.

As a whole, Ward 5 contains roughly 50 percent of all industrial land in D.C. and has relatively low tree coverage on par with Wards 1, 2 and 6.

It is also the third-poorest ward in the district, and Shaw says it would be even less affluent if not for the recent development of neighborhoods like NoMa.

Wards 7 and 8 also tell a story. Despite being the two poorest in the district, they are the third and fourth best in terms of tree coverage. In the first half of the 20th century, Shaw says, the neighborhoods east of the Anacostia River were mostly white. After the integration of public schools and the 1968 riots, though, white people largely left Southeast D.C., and a lack of economic opportunities and investment left residents struggling.

Both wards are heavily residentially zoned, as they have been since they were predominantly white, and the higher tree coverage is a remnant of that zoning.

## Real-life impact ##

Washington, D.C. is hardly the only city with a disparity in tree coverage. In fact, it’s more green than many major American cities. The district has over 32,000 trees per square mile; Philadelphia, New York and Los Angeles all have about 20,000.

Anthony David Jr., Empower DC’s environmental justice organizer, researched the relationship between historic redlining policies and the urban heat island effect in Harrisburg, Pa. when he was in college, and he found a similar trend: tree canopy coverage was concentrated in rich, mostly white neighborhoods.

Washington, D.C. is a unique case because of its high overall tree coverage, David says, but it still suffers from similar issues. 

The urban heat island effect occurs when urban areas see hotter temperatures than nearby rural areas due largely to reduced natural landscape. According to a 2018 study from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, neighborhoods in Northeast D.C. like Ivy City and Brentwood can get nearly 20 degrees hotter during summer days than the coolest parts of the district.

These issues often go overlooked for a few reasons. One, because the district has a lot of trees overall, people tend to believe that applies to every area of the district.

“We have Rock Creek Park… we have all these federal lands that have a lot of tree coverage,” David says. “So when you start to talk about trees, people are going to kind of look at you sideways because they’re like, ‘Oh, we have trees.’”

The Department of Energy and Environment has published several [climate resilience plans](https://doee.dc.gov/climateready) in the past decade, and some of those plans have focused on reducing the urban heat island effect, but David says he wants to see more focus both on tree equity and on “forgotten areas” like Ivy City.

The lack of tree coverage that Ivy City faces, David says, is emblematic of the lack of attention that it and similar neighborhoods get. Because few people visit the area (relative to areas like Georgetown and the National Mall), he says, the DC government lacks the incentive to invest in it.

“These are historically black neighborhoods and some of the oldest neighborhoods as well,” David says. “But they have always just been seen as a place to dump any industrial operations just to make profit for the rest of the city.”
